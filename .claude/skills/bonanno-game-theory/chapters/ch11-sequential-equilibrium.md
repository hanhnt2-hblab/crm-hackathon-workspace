# Chapter 11: Sequential Equilibrium

## Core Idea
Discipline off-path beliefs by requiring them to be the **limit** of Bayesian beliefs generated by completely mixed strategies converging to `σ`. This finally **does** refine subgame-perfect equilibrium — at the cost of a condition that Kreps and Wilson themselves, and Osborne–Rubinstein, call unnatural and opaque.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The diagnosis** (§11.1): weak sequential equilibrium's only restriction on beliefs is Bayesian updating at *reached* information sets. At unreached sets **any** beliefs are allowed — *even beliefs flatly at odds with `σ`*.
  - The clean illustration (Fig 11.1): with `σ = (c, d, f)` and `μ(u) = 1`, Player 3's information set `{s,t,u}` is unreached so Bayesian updating says nothing. But reaching `u` requires passing through Player 2's node, where `σ` has her play `d` **with probability 1** — making `u` impossible. The belief contradicts the very strategy it is paired with.

- **Why completely mixed strategies solve the problem**: if `σ(a) > 0` for **every** choice `a`, then every information set is reached with positive probability, so Bayesian updating yields **unique** beliefs everywhere. Consistency is then unambiguous.
  - Worked instance (Fig 11.1): with Player 1 playing `(a: p_a, b: p_b, c: 1−p_a−p_b)` and Player 2 playing `(d: p_d, e: 1−p_d)`, Bayesian updating forces
    ```
    μ(s) = p_a / D,    μ(t) = p_b p_d / D,    μ(u) = p_b(1−p_d) / D
    where D = p_a + p_b p_d + p_b(1−p_d)
    ```

- **Consistency** (Def 11.1, Kreps–Wilson 1982): an assessment `(σ, μ)` is **consistent** if there is a sequence of **completely mixed** strategy profiles `σ₁, σ₂, …` such that
  1. `lim_{n→∞} σₙ = σ`, and
  2. letting `μₙ` be the **unique** system of beliefs obtained from `σₙ` by Bayesian updating, `lim_{n→∞} μₙ = μ`.
  - The idea in one sentence: `(σ, μ)` is consistent if some completely mixed profile *arbitrarily close* to `σ` generates Bayesian beliefs *arbitrarily close* to `μ`.
  - **How to prove consistency**: exhibit a sequence. Usually put `1/n`-order probabilities on the choices that `σ` gives probability 0, compute `μₙ` by Bayesian updating, take limits. Relatively easy.
  - **How to prove INCONSISTENCY**: you must show that **every** possible sequence fails. Considerably harder. The technique: express the required belief **ratios** as limits of probability ratios, then derive a contradiction between two such ratios.

- **Sequential equilibrium** (Def 11.2, Kreps–Wilson 1982): an assessment `(σ, μ)` that is **consistent** (Def 11.1) **and sequentially rational** (Def 10.2).

- **Remark 11.1**: consistency implies Bayesian updating at reached information sets, so **every sequential equilibrium is a weak sequential equilibrium.**

- **Theorem 11.1 (Kreps–Wilson 1982)**: if `(σ, μ)` is a sequential equilibrium then `σ` **is a subgame-perfect equilibrium**. — This is the goal of Chapters 10–11 achieved.

- **Theorem 11.2 (Kreps–Wilson 1982)**: every finite extensive-form game with cardinal payoffs has **at least one** sequential equilibrium. (Together with Remark 11.1, this is what gives Ch 10's Theorem 10.2.)

- **The two objections to consistency** (§11.3):
  1. **Practical**: computationally hard to prove — you must construct a sequence of completely mixed strategies, compute the Bayesian beliefs, and take two limits. It gets worse as the game grows.
  2. **Conceptual**: it is not clear how to *interpret or justify* taking limits of sequences of strategies and beliefs.
  - Kreps and Wilson (1982, p. 876): *"we do so with some doubts of our own concerning what `ought' to be the definition of a consistent assessment that, with sequential rationality, will give the `proper' definition of a sequential equilibrium."*
  - Osborne and Rubinstein (1994, p. 225): *"We do not find the consistency requirement to be natural, since it is stated in terms of limits; it appears to be a rather opaque technical assumption."*

- **What sequential equilibrium still cannot do**: it eliminates **strictly** dominated choices at information sets (even zero-probability ones), but **not weakly dominated** ones.
  - **Trembling-hand perfect equilibrium** (Selten, 1975 — chronologically *earlier* than sequential equilibrium, by the same author who introduced subgame-perfection) is a **strict refinement** of sequential equilibrium that does eliminate weakly dominated strategies. Outside the book's scope (see van Damme 2002).

## Key Concepts
- **Completely mixed strategy profile** — `σ(a) > 0` for every choice `a` at every information set; makes every information set reachable.
- **Consistent assessment** — the limit condition of Def 11.1; the extension of Bayesian updating to zero-probability information sets.
- **Belief ratio technique** — since `μₙ(x)/μₙ(y)` equals a ratio of products of the `σₙ` probabilities (the common denominator cancels), inconsistency proofs work by comparing two such ratios.

## Reference Tables

The refinement hierarchy (Fig 11.5):

```
Nash equilibria
  ⊃ Subgame-perfect equilibria
      ⊃ Sequential equilibria                     (Thm 11.1)
          ⊂ Weak sequential equilibria            (Remark 11.1)
              ⊃ Trembling-hand perfect equilibria (Selten 1975)
```

Precisely: **Sequential ⊆ Subgame-perfect ⊆ Nash**, and **Sequential ⊆ Weak sequential ⊆ Nash**, while Subgame-perfect and Weak sequential merely **overlap** (Ch 10 Fig 10.10).

What each notion eliminates:

| Notion | Strictly dominated choices at unreached information sets | Beliefs contradicting `σ` | Weakly dominated choices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subgame-perfect | ✗ | — | ✗ |
| Weak sequential | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |
| **Sequential** | ✓ | **✓** | ✗ |
| Trembling-hand perfect | ✓ | ✓ | **✓** |

## Worked Example

**1 — Proving consistency by exhibiting a sequence (Fig 11.2).** Claim: `σ = (c, d, f)` with `μ = (s: ⅜, t: ⅝)` is consistent. Take

```
σₙ = ( a: 3/n,  b: 5/n,  c: 1 − 8/n  |  d: 1 − 1/n,  e: 1/n  |  f: 1 − 1/n,  g: 1/n )
```

*Condition (1)*: `3/n, 5/n, 1/n → 0` and `1 − 8/n, 1 − 1/n → 1`, so `lim σₙ = (c, d, f) = σ` ✓.

*Condition (2)*: Bayesian updating gives

```
μₙ(s) = (3/n) / [ 3/n + (5/n)(1 − 1/n) ]  →  3/8
μₙ(t) = (5/n)(1 − 1/n) / [ 3/n + (5/n)(1 − 1/n) ]  →  5/8
```

So `lim μₙ = (s: ⅜, t: ⅝) = μ` ✓. **Consistent.** Notice the design: the `3:5` ratio in the numerators is chosen to produce the target belief ratio `3:5`.

**2 — Proving INCONSISTENCY by contradiction between two ratios (Fig 11.3).** Claim: the assessment
```
σ = (a:1, b:0, c:0, d:0 | T:1, B:0 | e:0, f:1 | L:1, R:0)      μ = (r:½, s:½ ; t:⅕, u:⅖, v:⅖)
```
is **not** consistent. Suppose it were. Then some completely mixed sequence `σₙ` (with probabilities `p_n^a, p_n^b, …`) converges to `σ`, so

```
p_n^x → 0  for x ∈ {b, c, d, B, e, R}        p_n^x → 1  for x ∈ {a, T, f, L}
```

Bayesian updating gives (writing only what matters — the shared denominators cancel in ratios):

```
μₙ(r) = p_n^b / (p_n^b + p_n^c)          μₙ(s) = p_n^c / (p_n^b + p_n^c)
μₙ(t) ∝ p_n^b p_n^B + p_n^d              μₙ(u) ∝ p_n^c p_n^B p_n^f              μₙ(v) ∝ p_n^d
```

**Ratio 1** (from `μ(r) = μ(s) = ½`):
```
μₙ(s)/μₙ(r) = p_n^c / p_n^b   →   μ(s)/μ(r) = (½)/(½) = 1
```
So `p_n^c / p_n^b → 1`.

**Ratio 2** (from `μ(t) = ⅕`, `μ(u) = ⅖`):
```
μₙ(u)/μₙ(t)  →  μ(u)/μ(t) = (⅖)/(⅕) = 2
```
But `μₙ(u)/μₙ(t)` involves `(p_n^c p_n^f)/p_n^b` (approximately, once the `p_n^B`-carrying terms are compared), and

```
lim (p_n^c p_n^f / p_n^b) = (lim p_n^c/p_n^b)(lim p_n^f) = (1)(0) = 0   ≠  2
```

(Here `p_n^f → 0` in the relevant comparison term.) **Contradiction — the assessment is inconsistent.** This is the whole technique: *two belief ratios impose two incompatible limits on the same probability ratios.*

**3 — A full sequential equilibrium (Fig 11.4).** Claim:
```
σ = (L:0, M:0, R:1 | A:½, B:½ | c:1, d:0)      μ = (x:¼, y:¾ ; u:⅐, v:3⁄7, w:3⁄7)
```

*Sequential rationality.* Player 3 at `{u,v,w}`:
```
c: ⅐(5) + 3⁄7(1) + 3⁄7(2) = 2          d: ⅐(2) + 3⁄7(2) + 3⁄7(2) = 2
```
Indifferent → `c` is optimal (as would be `d` or any mix). Player 2 at `{x,y}`, given Player 3 plays `c`:
```
A: ¼(5) + ¾(3) = 3.5                   B: ¼(2) + ¾(4) = 3.5
```
Indifferent → the mixture `(A:½, B:½)` is optimal. Player 1 at the root:
```
R: 3        L: ½(4) + ½(1) = 2.5        M: ½(0) + ½(6) = 3
```
`R` is optimal (as would be any mix of `M` and `R`) ✓.

*Consistency.* Take
```
σₙ = ( L: 1/n,  M: 3/n,  R: 1 − 4/n  |  A: ½, B: ½  |  c: 1 − 1/n,  d: 1/n )
```
Clearly `lim σₙ = σ`. And the resulting `μₙ` is a **constant** sequence — the `1:3` ratio on `L:M` gives `μ(x) = ¼, μ(y) = ¾`, and the products `(1/n)(½)`, `(3/n)(½)`, `(3/n)(½)` give `⅐ : 3⁄7 : 3⁄7` at `{u,v,w}` — which trivially converges to `μ` ✓. **Sequential equilibrium.**

Compare Ch 10 Fig 10.3: the *same* strategy profile with `μ = (x:¼, y:¾ ; u:⅕, v:⅗, w:⅕)` was shown sequentially rational there. Consistency is what selects **which** of those belief systems is admissible.

**4 — A weakly dominated choice surviving as a sequential equilibrium (Fig 11.6).** A simultaneous 2×2 game rendered as a tree: Player 1 picks `A`/`B`, Player 2 (one information set `{x,y}`) picks `C`/`D`.

```
(A,C) → (0,0)    (A,D) → (2,2)    (B,C) → (1,3)    (B,D) → (1,3)
```

There are two Nash (and subgame-perfect) equilibria: `(A,D)` and `(B,C)`. But **`C` is weakly dominated** for Player 2. The *only* belief rationalizing `C` is that Player 1 chose `B` with **probability 1** — attach any positive probability, however small, to `A`, and `D` becomes the unique sequentially rational choice.

Nevertheless `σ = (B, C)` with `μ = (x:0, y:1)` **is** a sequential equilibrium. Consistency via

```
σₙ = ( A: 1/n,  B: 1 − 1/n  |  C: 1 − 1/n,  D: 1/n )     ⟹     μₙ = (x: 1/n, y: 1 − 1/n)  →  (0, 1)
```

and sequential rationality is immediate. **Sequential equilibrium cannot rule this out** — which is why trembling-hand perfection exists.

## Key Takeaways
1. The gap weak sequential equilibrium leaves is beliefs that **contradict the strategy profile** at unreached information sets. Consistency is the patch.
2. **Completely mixed ⟹ every information set reached ⟹ unique Bayesian beliefs.** That observation is the entire motivation for the limit definition.
3. To prove **consistency**, construct the sequence: put `k/n`-order weights on zero-probability choices, choosing the constants `k` so the resulting belief **ratios** match `μ`. Then take limits.
4. To prove **inconsistency**, work with **belief ratios** — the denominators cancel — and derive a contradiction between two of them. Never try to enumerate sequences.
5. **Sequential ⊆ Subgame-perfect** (Thm 11.1) and **existence holds** (Thm 11.2). The Ch 10 program succeeds.
6. **Every sequential equilibrium is weak sequential** (Remark 11.1), but not conversely; consistency is strictly stronger than Bayesian updating at reached sets.
7. Consistency is **easy to verify, hard to refute, and impossible to interpret.** Both the original authors and Osborne–Rubinstein say so in print. Treat that as licence to prefer the Ch 12 characterization.
8. Indifference at an information set is what makes many belief systems sequentially rational; **consistency is the tie-breaker** that decides which are admissible.
9. Sequential equilibrium kills **strictly** dominated choices, not **weakly** dominated ones. If you need the latter, you need trembling-hand perfection (Selten 1975).
